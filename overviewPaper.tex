\begin{abstract}
Mesoscale structures in the tropical atmosphere, from cloud clusters to regions of weak surface winds, are thought to influence the tropical energy budget and circulation, yet remain difficult to observe comprehensively. Here, we present the PERCUSION campaign, which in August and September 2024 used the German High Altitude and Long Range Research Aircraft (HALO), configured as a flying cloud observatory, to sample the Atlantic Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and its adjacent subsiding regions. During 23 research flights, based first in the eastern Atlantic (Sal, Cabo Verde) and then in the western Atlantic (Barbados), HALO followed a generic flight strategy: circular flight paths with regular dropsonde releases were placed at the edges and in the centre of the ITCZ, identified as the region of high column water vapour (CWV). Of the 90 dropsonde circles, 87 yielded profiles of area-averaged vertical velocity. The observations span the full transition from the dry, subsiding regions outside the ITCZ to the moist, ascending regions inside it. Three case studies illustrate the data and the substantial day-to-day variability of the ITCZ's mesoscale structure, including a day on which high CWV coincided with little deep convection in the ITCZ centre. The resulting dataset provides a benchmark for testing theories and for evaluating satellite retrievals and storm-resolving simulations.

The Global Atmospheric Research Program (GARP) Atlantic Tropical Experiment (GATE), conducted in 1974, was the largest atmospheric field study in history \citep{zhangRelocationGATEPacific2022}.
It set out to ``explore the mechanism by which solar heat stored in the tropical oceans drives the global circulation of the atmosphere, and to incorporate this mechanism into numerical models'' \citep{kuettnerGeneralDescriptionCentral1974}.
A key motivation of GATE was the recognition that deep convective clouds, while being fundamental to the tropical circulation \citep{riehlHeatBalanceEquatorial1958}, ``slip through the mesh'' of the synoptic-scale observing network \citep{kuettnerGeneralDescriptionCentral1974}.
To analyse the effect of deep convective clouds, or smaller-scale weather systems, on the large-scale circulation was therefore one of the primary objectives.
The results of GATE revealed that convective and large-scale processes are not well separated, but rather complex structures on the mesoscale exist that need to be accounted for \citep{houzejr.ConvectionGATE1981}.
Despite these findings, the assumption that small and large spatial scales can be clearly separated has remained a central tenet of the theoretical understanding of tropical climate, as encapsulated by model parameterisations of convection that largely neglect mesoscale processes governing convective organisation.
This framework has captured many aspects of anthropogenic climate change.
However, increasing regional discrepancies between observed and simulated changes have renewed attention on mesoscale processes and their coupling to both small and large scales \citep{shawOtherClimateCrisis2025}.

Fifty years after the completion of GATE, the Organized Convection and EarthCARE Studies over the Tropical Atlantic (ORCESTRA) campaign \citep{stevensORCESTRAOrganizedConvection2026} returned to the tropical Atlantic, focusing on mesoscale structures and the processes that govern their formation and evolution.
Taking place from August to September 2024, the campaign involved three research aircraft (\textit{citeBony}), a research vessel \citep{klocke40DaysNights2026} and two ground-based observatories \citep{winklerRAPSODIRadiosondeAtmospheric2026}. % TODO: add Sandrine's paper if available
It also coordinated measurements with the Earth Cloud, Aerosol and Radiation Explorer (EarthCARE) satellite \citep{illingworthEarthCARESatelliteNext2015}, which was launched less than three months before the start of ORCESTRA.
Together, these platforms sampled the Atlantic Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and the adjacent regions of subsidence from the ocean floor to the top of the atmosphere.

ORCESTRA comprised eight sub-campaigns.
This paper summarises PERCUSION (Persistent EarthCARE Underflight Studies of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and Organised Convection), the sub-campaign that utilised the German High Altitude and Long Range Research Aircraft (HALO).
The instrumentation of HALO was designed to resemble that of EarthCARE, and the calibration and validation of EarthCARE were a central component of PERCUSION.
This part of PERCUSION, including dedicated research flights in the extratropics, is described by \cite{grossPersistentEarthCAREUnderflight2026}.
Here, we focus on the research flights in and around the ITCZ conducted as part of ORCESTRA.
During ORCESTRA, HALO provided vertically resolved ``snapshots'' of the mesoscale structure of the atmospheric circulation and thermodynamic state of the ITCZ.
This was enabled by its high-altitude and long-range capabilities, together with its configuration as a cloud observatory \citep{stevensHighAltitudeLongRangeAircraft2019, bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019, georgeJOANNEJointDropsonde2021}.

The observations collected during PERCUSION provide a benchmark not only for satellite observations but also for limited-area and global storm-resolving models \citep[e.g.,][]{Klocke2017a, satohGlobalCloudResolvingModels2019, fievetTropicalConvectionStormresolving2026}.
The close link to both satellite measurements and modelling efforts contributes to the calibration and validation of satellite data and models.
It also enables the results to be extrapolated beyond the campaign period and study region, a common challenge in targeted field campaigns.

The scientific questions at the heart of the PERCUSION campaign focus on how strongly the ``inner life'' of the Atlantic ITCZ \citep{windmillerInnerLifeAtlantic2024} shapes its outer life.
More specifically, we examine how mesoscale structures of clouds, precipitation, surface winds, humidity, and aerosol particles impact the energy flows, both radiative and through the atmospheric circulation, in the deep tropics.

One pathway through which mesoscale structures affect energy flows is by modifying cloud properties and the associated vertical motion profiles.
Top-heavy vertical motion profiles, such as those produced by mesoscale convective systems, export energy more efficiently than bottom-heavy profiles, such as those associated with isolated deep convective cells \citep[e.g.,][]{Houze2004}.
It has been suggested that the vertical motion profile of convection also depends on the meridional structure of the ITCZ \citep{masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}.
Convection occurring within regions of strong gradients in column water vapour that mark the edges of the deep tropics \citep{Mapes2018, masunagaMechanismMaintenanceSharp2020} can differ substantially from convection occurring near the centre of the ITCZ.
Similarly, the zonal organisation of the ITCZ is associated with contrasting vertical motion profiles \citep{TODOpoujolBonyInRevision}. 
% TODO: replace citation key; preprint must be available at submission
These differences may even reverse the sign of the net energy export by the circulation, potentially explaining regional differences in energy export efficiency identified in reanalysis data \citep{backGeographicVariabilityExport2006}.
Recent work on the doldrums further illustrates the uncertainty surrounding the vertical motion field in the ITCZ.
These mesoscale regions of weak surface winds have traditionally been explained as a consequence of the rising air in the ITCZ.
Yet, although they lie in this region of mean ascent, they appear to occur when the air is subsiding \citep{windmillerCalmVariableInner2024}.

Mesoscale structures also influence the thermodynamic properties of the atmosphere. Convective aggregation affects entrainment and detrainment \citep{beckerEstimatingBulkEntrainment2018}, thereby altering the vertical distribution of humidity and air temperature \citep[e.g.,][]{singhInfluenceEntrainmentThermal2013}.
Changes in aggregation are further linked to systematic changes in clouds and radiation: more aggregated convection is typically associated with fewer high clouds and a drier troposphere, resulting in increased radiative cooling \citep[e.g.,][]{Tobin2012, bonyObservedModulationTropical2020}.
In the tropical Atlantic, radiation is influenced by aerosol particles transported within the Saharan Air Layer, whose dust and humidity modify radiative heating rates and may affect cloud formation \citep{gutlebenImpactsWaterVapor2019}.

The PERCUSION campaign aimed to quantify the effects of mesoscale structures and to improve the understanding of the processes that drive them. Tropical convection is known to be organised by large-scale disturbances such as equatorial waves \citep[e.g.,][]{kiladisConvectivelyCoupledEquatorial2009} and the Madden--Julian Oscillation \citep{maddenDetection4050Day1971, maddenDescriptionGlobalScaleCirculation1972}.
In addition to these large-scale drivers, processes acting on smaller spatial scales may contribute to the organisation of convection.
In idealised simulations with homogeneous initial and boundary conditions, convection that initially develops at random locations can spontaneously aggregate \citep{Held1993, Bretherton2005}. Depending on the model configuration and the stage of aggregation, a variety of feedbacks have been proposed to explain this behaviour, including convection-humidity feedbacks mediated by detrainment, cloud-radiative feedbacks, and radiatively driven circulations that transport humidity up-gradient \citep[reviewed by][]{Wing2017}.
On yet smaller spatial scales, clouds can trigger the formation of new convection through cold pools, linking successive generations of clouds and organising convection along gust fronts, notably in squall lines \citep{Rotunno1988, Weisman2004}, as well as along the edges of convectively active regions \citep{Nolan2016, windmillerConvectionEdge2019}.
Other processes have been argued to drive enhanced convection at the edges of the deep tropics \citep{TODOmasunagaBony2018, masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}. % TODO: add reference
Assessing the relative importance of these mechanisms in the real atmosphere remains an open challenge \citep{mullerSpontaneousAggregationConvective2022}.

To this end, PERCUSION collected data from the interior and margins of the Atlantic ITCZ, as well as from the surrounding regions of subsidence, first near Sal in the Republic of Cabo Verde in the eastern basin, and later east of Barbados in the western basin. Rather than targeting individual cloud systems, the flights systematically sampled the edges and centre of the ITCZ (Sect.~\ref{sec:campaign_design}).

A particular focus of PERCUSION was the measurement of quantities central to the tropical energy budget that are rarely accessible observationally. These include profiles of vertical motion derived from dropsonde circles \citep{bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019, gloecknerBEACHBarbadosEastern2025}, which constrain the overturning circulation of the tropical atmosphere, and direct observational estimates of convective entrainment, one of the most important yet poorly observed processes shaping convection and its environment \citep{sherwoodSpreadModelClimate2014}. PERCUSION also collected detailed observations of tropical ice clouds, including their microphysical properties and integrated quantities such as ice water path, providing key constraints on their radiative feedback, which remains a major uncertainty in climate projections \citep{sherwoodAssessmentEarthClimate2020}.
Many of these measurements rely on retrieval techniques and analysis methods that were further developed during and after the campaign, and the resulting datasets are being distributed using new approaches designed to facilitate community access and cross-platform analysis.

The remainder of the paper describes the measurement strategy (Section~\ref{sec:campaign_design}) and the instrumentation and data distribution (Section~\ref{sec:data}), followed by the implementation of the strategy during the campaign (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). Section~\ref{sec:overview_plots} shows how the observations sample the ITCZ and presents selected case studies, before concluding in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Campaign design}
\label{sec:campaign_design}

During ORCESTRA, PERCUSION followed a largely generic flight strategy to sample the Atlantic ITCZ. By this we mean that flight planning followed a predefined set of rules that were applied consistently throughout the campaign. The central idea was to sample the structure of the ITCZ in an ITCZ-based reference frame, i.e. to systematically sample the properties at the edges and in the centre of the ITCZ rather than, for example, targeting the locations of deepest convection. Following \cite{Mapes2018}, the ITCZ was primarily identified through its high column water vapour (CWV) content, with its edges defined either by the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour or by the strongest CWV gradient. In cases where the CWV field was fragmented, or where separate regions of high CWV occurred north of the ITCZ, surface wind direction was additionally considered following \cite{windmillerInnerLifeAtlantic2024}.
%The location and width of the Atlantic ITCZ can change by several degrees within the course of days \cite{Chen1982, Frank1983}. 

A summary of rules used for flight planning, together with an example flight, is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:generic_flight_east}. 
In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item flights were scheduled on days when an EarthCARE underpass was within operational reach,
\item flights took place during daytime, because several of the passive remote sensing instruments require sunlight; nighttime overpasses of EarthCARE were therefore not targeted,
\item HALO took the most direct route to the EarthCARE ground track after takeoff and remained on it, often for the majority of the flight, always for at least twenty minutes before and after the satellite underpass,
\item when the dominant CWV gradient was oriented north--south: dropsonde circles with a radius of about 130\,\unit{km} were placed at the southern and northern edges and in the centre of the ITCZ,
\item when the dominant CWV gradient was oriented east--west (occasionally in the western basin): dropsonde circles of the same size were arranged along this gradient,
\item the order in which the circles were flown was not fixed but depended on the timing of the EarthCARE underpass and the length of the flight,
\item whenever possible, HALO operated at the highest feasible altitude, ideally above the tropical high-cloud layer,
\item coordinated observations with other platforms were conducted whenever possible, including overpasses of and circles around RV METEOR and the SAFIRE ATR-42, the latter with a smaller radius (about 60\,\unit{km}) to optimise the comparison between the circle-averaged quantities and the ATR in situ measurements along its roughly 200\,\unit{km} long legs, as well as overpasses of the Cape Verde Atmospheric Observatory (CVAO) and the Barbados Cloud Observatory (BCO).
\end{itemize}

In these cases, the dropsonde circles were aligned east-west to sample along the strongest column water vapour gradients but without crossing the ITCZ.

The rationale behind the different elements of the flight strategy was as follows. Flying along the EarthCARE ground track served several purposes.
First, it contributed to the calibration and validation of EarthCARE observations and facilitated the extrapolation of our results to other regions and periods.
Second, HALO often flew along the track in both directions, although this became harder in the western basin. Together with the near-instantaneous cross-section from EarthCARE, this yielded up to three cross-sections of the ITCZ. Only at the underpass did HALO and EarthCARE sample the same location at the same time; elsewhere along the track, the cross-sections were taken up to several hours apart.
This configuration provides a means of assessing how strongly the mesoscale structure evolves on timescales of a few hours.
Third, flying directly to the EarthCARE ground track after takeoff, rather than taking the shortest route to the ITCZ edge, ensured that HALO also sampled the full transition from the subsiding region to the ITCZ centre.

The placement of dropsonde circles at the ITCZ edges, or along the dominant CWV gradient, was motivated by the desire to connect the observations as directly as possible to previous studies. In particular, the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour has been identified as a transition between the deep tropics and the surrounding subsiding regions, while edge intensification refers to precipitation maxima occurring preferentially near this boundary. Vertical velocity measurements in these regions therefore enable a direct comparison with the mechanisms discussed by \cite{Mapes2018}, \cite{masunagaMechanismMaintenanceSharp2020}, and \cite{masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}.

More generally, defining the ITCZ in terms of CWV provides a natural connection to idealised studies of convective self-aggregation \citep[e.g.,][]{TODOcraigMack2013, Wing2017a}, in which the moist margins mark the regions where horizontal upgradient moisture transport by a shallow circulation \citep{Muller2012}, if present, is expected to be most efficient. It also allows a direct comparison with the observation-based reconstruction of the overturning circulation in moisture space, including a shallow branch, by \cite{TODOschulzStevens2018}. They used five years of ground-based measurements at the BCO, exploiting the fact that the BCO samples different parts of moisture space over the course of the year. % TODO: add references

\subsection{Instrumentation}
\label{sec:instrumentation}

HALO operated in an updated version of the “flying cloud observatory” configuration described by \cite{stevensHighAltitudeLongRangeAircraft2019}. This configuration combines active and passive remote-sensing instruments with in situ measurements from dropsondes, providing a comprehensive view of the atmospheric state, including its thermodynamic and dynamical structure as well as the microphysical properties of aerosol particles, clouds, and precipitation. The complete configuration used during PERCUSION is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:instrumentation}.
% TODO: add HALO endurance sentence
A key component of the remote-sensing suite is the HALO Microwave Package (HAMP), which combines a suite of passive microwave radiometers with a cloud radar \citep{mechHAMPMicrowavePackage2014, ewaldCalibration35GHz2019}. The passive radiometers consist of three downward-looking modules with 26 frequencies in different bands between 22.24 and 183.31\,\unit{GHz} $\pm$ 12.5\,\unit{GHz} that sample water vapour and oxygen absorption bands, as well as window channels. These measurements provide information on the different thermodynamic phases of water and their vertically integrated amounts, such as column water vapour, liquid water path and ice water path. The nadir-pointing microwave cloud and precipitation radar operates at 35.5\,\unit{GHz} (Ka band) and is suitable for penetrating columns with heavy precipitation. It measures radar reflectivity, reflectivity-weighted Doppler velocity, and the linear-depolarisation ratio to determine target shape.

The lidar system WALES (WAter vapour Lidar Experiment in Space) was developed as an airborne demonstrator for a mission proposal within the European Space Agency's (ESA) Earth Explorer programme. WALES operates at four wavelengths near 935\,\unit{nm} to deliver profiles of the water vapour mixing ratio below the aircraft at a vertical resolution of [X]\,\unit{m} and a horizontal resolution of [Y]\,\unit{km}, using the DIfferential Absorption Lidar (DIAL) technique \citep{wirthAirborneMultiwavelengthWater2009}. Additionally, it has backscatter lidar channels at 532\,\unit{nm} and 1064\,\unit{nm}; at 532\,\unit{nm}, aerosol depolarisation and extinction can be retrieved using the High Spectral Resolution Lidar (HSRL) method. At 1064\,\unit{nm}, only attenuated backscatter measurements are possible. % TODO: WALES resolutions

Radiance and irradiance measurements are obtained using the Spectral Modular Airborne Radiation Measurement System (SMART), a suite of passive spectrometers measuring spectral irradiances and radiances within the visible and near-infrared wavelength ranges (0.3--2.2\,\unit{\mu m}) \citep{wendischAirborneSpectralAlbedometer2001, wendischACRIDICONCHUVACampaign2016}. The irradiance measurements allow the determination of the spectral cloud top albedo, while the spectral radiance observations are used for cloud flagging and to derive microphysical properties, including cloud optical depth and effective droplet radius.
The spectrometer of the Munich Aerosol Cloud Scanner (specMACS) complements these measurements by providing hyperspectral imaging in a wavelength range similar to that of SMART (0.4--2.5\,\unit{\mu m}; \citealp{ewaldDesignCharacterizationSpecMACS2016}), combined with measurements of four two-dimensional polarisation-resolving RGB cameras \citep{weberPolarizationUpgradeSpecMACS2024}, two downward-looking and two newly installed sideward-facing, such that an overall across-track field of view of 220$^{\circ}$ is covered.
The specMACS measurements allow the characterisation of clouds with respect to their macroscopic geometry and their microphysical structure in terms of the cloud droplet size distribution, cloud optical depth and thermodynamic phase.

This imaging capability is extended into the thermal infrared by the Video airbornE Longwave Observations within siX channels (VELOX) instrument, which captures high-resolution two-dimensional fields of upward terrestrial radiance across six spectral bands between 7.7 and 12.0\,\unit{\mu m}, providing information on cloud top brightness temperature, cloud mask/fraction, and cloud top altitude \citep{schaferVELOXNewThermal2022}.
To quantify the cloud radiative effect and the energy budget, HALO utilises the Broadband AirCrAft RaDiometer Instrumentation (BACARDI), which consists of two sets of pyranometers and pyrgeometers mounted on the upper and lower fuselage to measure upward and downward solar (0.3--3\,\unit{\mu m}) and thermal-infrared (3--100\,\unit{\mu m}) irradiances, respectively \citep{ehrlichNewAirborneBroadband2023}.

In situ observations are primarily obtained using dropsondes deployed with the Airborne Vertical Atmospheric Profiling System (AVAPS). Vaisala RD-41 dropsondes provide measurements of temperature, humidity, pressure, and GPS-derived horizontal winds throughout the atmospheric column. Additionally, for each circle, area-averaged estimates of large-scale divergence, vorticity and vertical velocity within the sampled air masses are calculated from horizontal winds \citep{bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019}.
A detailed description of the PERCUSION dropsonde dataset, including data processing, quality control, and the derivation of circle-averaged quantities, is provided by \cite{gloecknerBEACHBarbadosEastern2025}.
Additional in situ measurements of temperature, humidity, and pressure at flight level are provided by HALO's core instrument: BAHAMAS (BAsic HAlo Measurement And Sensor system; \citealp{krautstrunkTransitionFALCONHALO2012}).

\subsection{Data distribution}
\label{sec:data_distribution}

As part of PERCUSION, a decentralised data distribution strategy was introduced for HALO to ensure that scientific datasets are openly accessible, verifiable and resilient. Moving away from traditional central servers, this approach uses the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS), which operates on two core principles:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Content addressing: datasets are identified and referenced by a unique, cryptographically secure content identifier (CID) derived from the content itself, which acts as an immutable fingerprint. Any change to the data therefore results in a new CID, ensuring that any analysis can be traced back to exactly the data it used.
    \item Data lookup by CID in a peer-to-peer network: IPFS provides protocols to find and exchange data in a network. As the CID is sufficient to identify the data and verify their integrity, any computer can serve the data.
\end{itemize}

We first used the peer-to-peer capability of IPFS for on-site data sharing during the field campaign.
To increase retrievability and ensure long-term availability, we continue to use IPFS with multiple independent, well-connected server locations (currently the German Climate Computing Center (DKRZ), the Gesellschaft für wissenschaftliche Datenverarbeitung mbH Göttingen (GWDG) and other partner institutions), so that there is no single point of failure.
Furthermore, users can automatically cache and share retrieved datasets, providing fast access for repeated use.

To make the data findable, we built the ORCESTRA Data Browser (\url{https://browser.orcestra-campaign.org/}), which provides search and preview functionality for the datasets. The browser extracts the metadata on demand directly from the original datasets on IPFS, so that metadata do not have to be duplicated or maintained separately.
To further enhance scholarly traceability, DOIs with embedded CIDs can be provided for each dataset.

The remaining circle was flown to gain altitude. 

To enable an easy and reproducible analysis of the key elements of the flights, a flight segmentation system was developed. Based on that system, the individual flights were categorised manually. The system distinguishes between segments and events. Segments are defined by start and end timestamps (e.g. the flight along the EarthCARE ground track), while events are defined as specific occurrences during a flight (e.g. the time of the EarthCARE underpass) and marked by a single timestamp. Both segments and events are categorised according to their 'kind'. They are organised hierarchically and are not mutually exclusive. The results of the flight segmentation are provided in YAML format, which is both human- and machine-readable, can be read with standard tools in most programming languages, and can be easily accessed in Python via the \textit{pyorcestra} package.

Throughout the campaign, systematic adaptations to the generic flight strategy were necessary. These included adjustments to enable coordinated measurements with other platforms, responses to weather conditions (e.g.~avoiding severe thunderstorms), airspace restrictions, technical issues, on-board instrument calibration and validation, and targeted manoeuvres to test new retrieval techniques. While these factors led primarily to minor adjustments in the eastern basin, more substantial modifications were required in the western Atlantic. In particular, the combination of stricter airspace restrictions and a more variable CWV field necessitated changes to the sampling strategy. In this region, the strongest CWV gradients were frequently oriented in an east--west direction. In some cases, these gradients were either not clearly identifiable or located too far east to be reachable within the available flight time. Consequently, dropsonde circles in the western Atlantic were often aligned either east--west or in mixed east--west and north--south configurations. Nevertheless, even in cases with predominantly east--west sampling, parts of the flight track remained aligned in the north--south direction in order to maintain sufficient overlap with the EarthCARE ground track for calibration and validation purposes.

In the following, we present selected measurements collected during the 23 PERCUSION research flights. We first demonstrate that the observations sampled the Atlantic ITCZ, by examining the measurements in moisture space. We then illustrate the day-to-day variability of the ITCZ through three case studies: one at the edge of the ITCZ, and two near its centre, one characterised by deep convection and one with little deep convection and weak surface winds.

\subsection{Sampling the ITCZ} 

We begin by demonstrating that the research flights sampled the Atlantic ITCZ, including its centre, edges and adjacent subsiding regions. To this end, we examine the sampling relative to the moist margin, defined by the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour, and the interior of the ITCZ, defined by CWV values exceeding 48\,\unit{mm}. We then show that the observations capture the transition from subsiding to ascending conditions and from infrequent to frequent deep convection, corresponding to the transition from outside to inside the ITCZ.

\subsubsection{Sampling in moisture space}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:campaign_design}, the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour was used to guide the placement of dropsonde circles at the edges and within the ITCZ. Figure~\ref{fig:transition}a shows CWV calculated from the dropsondes released in these circles. Of the 87 dropsonde circles with sufficient data to calculate circle mean values (see above), 51 contain at least one dropsonde with CWV below 48\,\unit{mm} and at least one with CWV above 48\,\unit{mm}, indicating that they sampled the 48\,\unit{mm} contour. Of the remaining 36 circles, 35 contain only dropsondes with CWV values above 48\,\unit{mm} and only one contains exclusively dropsondes with CWV below 48\,\unit{mm}.

A comparison of the CWV ranges sampled during individual flights reveals a clear distinction between flights based in Sal and those based in Barbados. As described above, flights from Sal (all flights before 6 September 2024) typically included two circles at the ITCZ edges, one in the ITCZ centre, and one coordinated with the ATR. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}a, eight of the eleven flights in the eastern tropical Atlantic sampled both ITCZ edges and the ITCZ centre. This pattern changes markedly in the western basin, where the flights differed substantially (see Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). None of the flights sampled both ITCZ edges and the centre. Nevertheless, seven of the eleven flights included at least one circle at an ITCZ edge and one in the centre. Thus, the western flights also sampled the ITCZ structure in moisture space, albeit less systematically than those in the east.

While Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}a shows only the mean and range of CWV for each circle, Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}b shows the full distribution of CWV values measured by all dropsondes, together with the distribution measured by HAMP. It highlights that the observations were concentrated near the ITCZ edge and within the moist ITCZ. In particular, the distribution exhibits a pronounced peak near 57\,\unit{mm}, close to the peak at 55\,\unit{mm} reported by \cite{Mapes2018}. The dropsonde-derived CWV also shows a weaker secondary peak near 50\,\unit{mm}. This differs from \cite{Mapes2018}, who found a local minimum around 48\,\unit{mm}. This reflects our targeted sampling of the 48\,\unit{mm} contour. Consistent with our objectives, the majority of our observations were collected in the region of high CWV but also allow for analysis of the adjacent drier subtropical regions as our observations include CWV values as low as 30\,\unit{mm} and 24$\%$ of both the dropsonde and HAMP measurements fall below 48\,\unit{mm}.  
Finally, the close agreement between the CWV distributions derived from dropsondes and measured by HAMP indicates that the dropsonde circles adequately capture the variability observed throughout the flights.

In addition to the dropsonde and HAMP measurements, Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}b also shows the CWV distribution derived from WALES. We include only measurements for which at least 90\% of the profile below the aircraft contains valid data, a criterion met by about 22\% of the WALES observations. This relatively low yield is expected, as WALES is a nadir-pointing lidar, and cloud presence frequently prevents retrieval of water vapour profiles below cloud tops. Excluded profiles are assigned a nominal value of zero and the distribution is normalised over the full set of measurements, so that its amplitude reflects both the underlying CWV distribution and the fraction of usable retrievals. Its shape and peak location remain meaningful and comparable to the other distributions. The resulting distribution can be interpreted as representing clear-sky conditions: it agrees well with the dropsonde and HAMP distributions at low CWV values, but its peak is shifted to substantially lower CWV, near 45\,\unit{mm}, with increasingly few data points at higher CWV.

In summary, the observations sampled the moist peak, the edge of the tropical CWV distribution and the adjacent dry regions. The moist margins were sampled most systematically in the eastern basin, while observations in the western basin nevertheless captured much of the transition between dry and moist regions.


\subsubsection{ITCZ in moisture space}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/itcz_cwv_space.pdf}
    % TODO (figure): error bars in (b); axis label "upwelling longwave radiation flux" in (c); SST vs CWV depends on answer from Manfred (VELOX skin temperature).
    \caption{ITCZ in moisture space. (a) cloud occurrence frequency from HAMP radar (yellow to brown colours) and relative humidity from WALES (blue colours) as well as frequency of stereographically derived cloud top heights from specMACS (black contours: dotted 0.01\%, dashed 0.02\%, solid 0.04\%), (b) vertical velocity from dropsondes, and (c) IWP from HAMP passive radiometers and upwelling longwave radiation from BACARDI.}
    \label{fig:itcz_cwv}
\end{figure}

In the previous section, we showed that our measurements span a broad range of CWV values, from the relatively dry regions adjacent to the ITCZ, across its moist margins, and into its moist interior. An important remaining question is whether this CWV-based sampling corresponds to a comprehensive sampling of the ITCZ itself, that is, the transition between the dry, subsiding environment and the moist, ascending region.
To address this question, we examine a subset of our measurements in moisture space. We choose this representation rather than averaging measurements by latitude because CWV provides a dynamical definition of the moist tropics. Consequently, even when the ITCZ widens or narrows, shifts meridionally, or breaks up into more complex structures, deep convection is expected to be limited to regions of high CWV. 

Figure~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv} shows how cloudiness, humidity, vertical motion and radiation change across the transition into the ITCZ. Figure~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}a shows a simple estimate of cloud occurrence frequency derived from the HAMP radar, profiles of relative humidity from WALES, together with upwelling longwave radiative flux measured by BACARDI and ice water path (IWP) retrieved from the HAMP radiometers. All quantities are binned and averaged according to the CWV measured by HAMP using 1\,\unit{mm}-wide bins spanning 44\,\unit{mm} to 61\,\unit{mm}, corresponding to the 10th and 90th percentiles of the observed CWV distribution, respectively.

Cloud occurrence frequency is estimated as the fraction of observations with an equivalent radar reflectivity factor exceeding $1\times10^{-5}\,$\unit{mm^{6}\,m^{-3}}. As expected in the deep tropics, clouds become both deeper and more frequent with increasing CWV. Shallow trade-wind clouds first become detectable at around 50\,\unit{mm}. The relatively low occurrence frequency below this threshold may partly reflect the limited sensitivity of the radar to the smallest cloud droplets, which become more readily detectable as clouds deepen \citep{konowEURECLtSup2021}.

At the same time, the free troposphere transitions from being largely cloud-free, apart from frequent high clouds, to exhibiting cloud occurrence frequencies approaching 50,$\%$ throughout much of the atmospheric column. This transition accelerates for CWV values exceeding approximately 56\,\unit{mm}. The rapid increase in deep convective cloud occurrence at higher CWV is consistent with the increase in precipitation rates with column water vapour \citep[e.g.,][]{Bretherton2004, Peters2006}.

In addition to the peaks in cloud occurrence associated with trade-wind clouds and deep convection, a third maximum is apparent near 5500\,\unit{m}, reminiscent of the trimodal structure of tropical convection \citep{johnsonTrimodalCharacteristicsTropical1999}. Consistent with the increase in deep convection, upwelling longwave radiation decreases with increasing CWV, reflecting the growing influence of high, cold cloud tops. This decrease is closely mirrored by the increase in ice water path.

While these quantities primarily characterise cloudiness, Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv} also shows the vertical profile of relative humidity outside of clouds. Relative humidity is calculated from the water-vapour molecular density measured by WALES, temperature from the meteorological analysis included in the WALES product, and the Bolton formulation for the saturation vapour pressure over liquid water.

\subsubsection{Wind-still ITCZ}
Figure~\ref{fig:weak_winds} shows flight \textit{HALO-20240907a} on 7 September, based in Barbados. As in the previous case, three large dropsonde circles were placed along the EarthCARE track to sample the ITCZ's structure; however, the longer transit to the track meant that only a single north--south crossing could be flown. No coordination circle was flown, as the ATR remained in the eastern Atlantic throughout the campaign and the RV METEOR was not yet within reach.

As in Section~\ref{sec:cloudy_itcz}, we here focus on the dropsonde circle flown in the centre of the ITCZ. CWV in this circle is comparably high, at 60.16\,\unit{mm}, yet both the vertical velocity profile -- close to zero up to about 5\,\unit{km} -- and the radar reflectivity indicate very little convective activity: no convection extended throughout the column, and what did occur remained largely confined below about 7\,\unit{km}.

Rather than deep convection, the centre of the ITCZ on this flight was characterised by an extended region of low surface winds, consistent with doldrums conditions. The dropsonde-derived mean wind speed in the lowest 100\,\unit{m} is 2.5\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}, below the commonly used doldrums threshold of 3\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}; indeed, 9 of the 13 dropsondes released in the circle measured near-surface wind speeds below this threshold. This is also consistent with the LAM simulation, which shows extended regions of low wind speed across the ITCZ on that day. 

Low surface wind speeds were also evident in the specMACS measurements. Using a newly developed retrieval, specMACS can, under suitable conditions, estimate surface wind speed from the specular reflection of sunlight on the ocean surface: lower wind speeds produce a smoother sea surface and thus a more pronounced specular reflection. This is illustrated by the two near-cloud-free specMACS images in Fig.~\ref{fig:weak_winds}d: the image taken at 16:06:29 UTC shows comparatively weak specular reflection, corresponding to a retrieved wind speed of 3.5\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}, while the image at 16:56:29 UTC shows a far more pronounced reflection, corresponding to a retrieved wind speed below 1.0\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}.

Convective activity in the centre of the ITCZ was limited but not absent. A further specMACS RGB image, taken at 16:16:03 UTC, shows one of the convective clouds that reached up to about 7\,\unit{km} altitude; the accompanying specMACS retrievals in Fig.~\ref{fig:weak_winds}d illustrate its macro- and microphysical properties. 
The cloud top heights are derived using a stereographic approach \citep{Koelling2019,Volkmer2024a}.
Liquid cloud properties in terms of the effective droplet size and width of the cloud droplet size distribution (in particular effective radius (reff) and effective variance (veff)) are derived from polarised measurements of the cloudbow \citep{portgeHighspatialresolutionRetrievalCloud2023}.
Since the microphysics retrieval requires the observation of cloud targets under scattering angles of $135 ^{\circ}$ to $165 ^{\circ}$, the reff and veff are derived only for parts of the image. 
The large cloud-top effective radii (reff between about 15\,\unit{\mu m} and 30\,\unit{\mu m}) and broad droplet size distributions (large veff) hint at the presence of rain beneath, consistent with the radar measurements at the time the image was taken.
The contrast between the case studies shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:deep_conv_itcz} and \ref{fig:weak_winds} highlights the day-to-day variability of the ITCZ captured by our measurements.

\conclusions
\label{sec:conclusion}



% Summary

PERCUSION (Persistent EarthCARE Underflight Studies of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and Organised Convection) was a measurement campaign centred on the German research aircraft HALO.
It was one of the eight sub-campaigns of the ORCESTRA (Organized Convection and EarthCARE Studies over the Tropical Atlantic) campaign, conducted over the tropical Atlantic in August and September 2024. 
PERCUSION's central goal was to collect measurements that allow us to address how the mesoscale structure of clouds, precipitation, surface winds, humidity and aerosols shapes processes on both the cloud and the large scale.
% TODO: consider mentioning focus on ITCZ energetics and sampling in moisture space

To this end, HALO systematically sampled the ITCZ -- identified primarily as a region of high column water vapour -- together with its edges and the adjacent subsiding regions, during a total of 23 research flights. Eleven of these flights collected data in the eastern tropical Atlantic (based in Sal, Cabo Verde), eleven in the western tropical Atlantic (based in Barbados), and one transfer flight sampled across the basin.

% Comment on the paragraph below: Potentially the sentence trying to summarize all measured and retrieved variables may be split in two (or more). In my oppinion it is currently unprecise in terms of which quantities are vertical profiles and which are horizontally resolved. Some products may also be retrieved from multiple techniques (e.g. cloud microphysics in the vertical from combined lidar-radar retrievals and horizontally resolved from polarized specMACS measurements).
Taking advantage of HALO's ability to fly at or even above the tropical high-cloud layer, the active and passive remote-sensing instruments on board (HAMP, WALES, specMACS, SMART, VELOX and BACARDI) provided vertical profiles of radar reflectivity, water vapour, aerosol backscatter and cloud micro- and macrophysical properties, as well as two-dimensional fields of brightness temperature, broadband irradiances, spectral radiances and integrated quantities such as column water vapour and ice water path. These were complemented by in situ profiles of temperature, humidity, pressure and horizontal wind from dropsondes, released in 93 circles across the campaign, 87 of which yielded area-averaged profiles of divergence and vertical motion.

Combining all flights into a mean picture of the deep tropics, we find that the observations span the full transition from the dry, subsiding, shallow cloud dominated environment into the moist, ascending centre of the ITCZ, where deep convection is frequent, ice water path is large and upwelling longwave radiation is correspondingly reduced. Within the ITCZ, cloud occurrence peaks at three levels---below about 2\,km, near the freezing level, and close to flight altitude---consistent with the well known trimodal structure of tropical convection. Beyond the mean, our measurements also capture the day-to-day variability of the mesoscale structure of the ITCZ, including days on which high column water vapour coincided with almost no deep convection.

% Implications
The collected data now enable us to address some of the fundamental questions raised in the introduction: do differences in mesoscale cloud, humidity and aerosol structure imprint themselves on the vertical motion profile and on the thermodynamic properties of the atmosphere? And, conversely, do we find evidence that mesoscale organisation is driven by the mechanisms hypothesised on the basis of idealised simulations?

% Limitations
While the observations capture both the mean and the variability of mesoscale structure in the ITCZ, an aircraft campaign necessarily samples one region in one season. Three elements of PERCUSION help to alleviate this limitation. Flying along EarthCARE ground tracks ties our high-resolution observations to a global satellite record, so that the relationships we identify locally can be tested elsewhere and at other times. The observations provide a benchmark for limited-area and global storm-resolving models, which in turn provide a physically consistent extension of the observations in space and time. And the coordination with the other ORCESTRA platforms---RV METEOR, the SAFIRE ATR-42, and the Barbados and Cape Verde observatories---places the aircraft snapshots within a longer and more continuous record of the same atmosphere.

% Future Work
As with any field campaign, collecting data is only the first step towards answering the underlying research questions. The next step is to analyse it. As well as addressing the questions that motivated the campaign, we hope that the data---and the decentralised way in which we distribute it---will also support questions that we did not anticipate, just as the observations collected during the GATE campaign now serve as a reference for our own, separated from them by fifty years of global warming.
