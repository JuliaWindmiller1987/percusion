\begin{abstract}
Mesoscale structures in the tropical atmosphere, from cloud clusters to regions of weak surface winds, are thought to influence the tropical energy budget and circulation, yet remain difficult to observe comprehensively. Here, we present the PERCUSION campaign, which in August and September 2024 used the German High Altitude and Long Range Research Aircraft (HALO), configured as a flying cloud observatory, to sample the Atlantic Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and its adjacent subsiding regions. During 23 research flights, based first in the eastern Atlantic (Sal, Cabo Verde) and then in the western Atlantic (Barbados), HALO followed a generic flight strategy: circular flight paths with regular dropsonde releases were placed at the edges and in the centre of the ITCZ, identified as the region of high column water vapour (CWV). Of the 90 dropsonde circles, 87 yielded profiles of area-averaged vertical velocity. The observations span the full transition from the dry, subsiding regions outside the ITCZ to the moist, ascending regions inside it. Three case studies illustrate the data and the substantial day-to-day variability of the ITCZ's mesoscale structure, including a day on which high CWV coincided with little deep convection in the ITCZ centre. The resulting dataset provides a benchmark for testing theories and for evaluating satellite retrievals and storm-resolving simulations.

The Global Atmospheric Research Program (GARP) Atlantic Tropical Experiment (GATE), conducted in 1974, was the largest atmospheric field study in history \citep{zhangRelocationGATEPacific2022}.
It set out to ``explore the mechanism by which solar heat stored in the tropical oceans drives the global circulation of the atmosphere, and to incorporate this mechanism into numerical models'' \citep{kuettnerGeneralDescriptionCentral1974}.
A key motivation of GATE was the recognition that deep convective clouds, while being fundamental to the tropical circulation \citep{riehlHeatBalanceEquatorial1958}, ``slip through the mesh'' of the synoptic-scale observing network \citep{kuettnerGeneralDescriptionCentral1974}.
To analyse the effect of deep convective clouds, or smaller-scale weather systems, on the large-scale circulation was therefore one of the primary objectives.
The results of GATE revealed that convective and large-scale processes are not well separated, but rather complex structures on the mesoscale exist that need to be accounted for \citep{houzejr.ConvectionGATE1981}.
Despite these findings, the assumption that small and large spatial scales can be clearly separated has remained a central tenet of the theoretical understanding of tropical climate, as encapsulated by model parameterisations of convection that largely neglect mesoscale processes governing convective organisation.
This framework has captured many aspects of anthropogenic climate change.
However, increasing regional discrepancies between observed and simulated changes have renewed attention on mesoscale processes and their coupling to both small and large scales \citep{shawOtherClimateCrisis2025}.

Fifty years after the completion of GATE, the Organized Convection and EarthCARE Studies over the Tropical Atlantic (ORCESTRA) campaign \citep{stevensORCESTRAOrganizedConvection2026} returned to the tropical Atlantic, focusing on mesoscale structures and the processes that govern their formation and evolution.
Taking place from August to September 2024, the campaign involved three research aircraft (\textit{citeBony}), a research vessel \citep{klocke40DaysNights2026} and two ground-based observatories \citep{winklerRAPSODIRadiosondeAtmospheric2026}. % TODO: add Sandrine's paper if available
It also coordinated measurements with the Earth Cloud, Aerosol and Radiation Explorer (EarthCARE) satellite \citep{illingworthEarthCARESatelliteNext2015, wehrEarthCAREMissionScience2023}, whose launch was coordinated with the ORCESTRA field activities.
Together, these platforms sampled the Atlantic Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and the adjacent regions of subsidence from the deep ocean through the stratosphere.

ORCESTRA comprised eight sub-campaigns.
This paper summarises PERCUSION (Persistent EarthCARE Underflight Studies of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and Organised Convection), the sub-campaign that utilised the German High Altitude and Long Range Research Aircraft (HALO).
The instrumentation of HALO was designed to resemble that of EarthCARE, and the calibration and validation of EarthCARE were a central component of PERCUSION.
This part of PERCUSION, including dedicated research flights in the extratropics, is described by \cite{grossPersistentEarthCAREUnderflight2026}.
Here, we focus on the research flights in and around the ITCZ conducted as part of ORCESTRA.
During ORCESTRA, HALO provided vertically resolved ``snapshots'' of the mesoscale structure of the atmospheric circulation and thermodynamic state of the ITCZ.
This was enabled by its high-altitude and long-range capabilities, together with its configuration as a cloud observatory \citep{stevensHighAltitudeLongRangeAircraft2019, bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019, georgeJOANNEJointDropsonde2021}.

The observations collected during PERCUSION provide a benchmark not only for satellite observations but also for limited-area and global storm-resolving models \citep[e.g.,][]{Klocke2017a, satohGlobalCloudResolvingModels2019, fievetTropicalConvectionStormresolving2026}.
The close link to both satellite measurements and modelling efforts contributes to the calibration and validation of satellite data and models.
It also enables the results to be extrapolated beyond the campaign period and study region, a common challenge in targeted field campaigns.

The scientific questions at the heart of the PERCUSION campaign focus on how strongly the ``inner life'' of the Atlantic ITCZ \citep{windmillerInnerLifeAtlantic2024} shapes its outer life.
More specifically, we examine how mesoscale structures of clouds, precipitation, surface winds, humidity, and aerosol particles impact the energy flows, both radiative and through the atmospheric circulation, in the deep tropics.

One pathway through which mesoscale structures affect energy flows is by modifying cloud properties and the associated vertical motion profiles.
Top-heavy vertical motion profiles, such as those produced by mesoscale convective systems, export energy more efficiently than bottom-heavy profiles, such as those associated with isolated deep convective cells \citep[e.g.,][]{Houze2004}.
It has been suggested that the vertical motion profile of convection also depends on the meridional structure of the ITCZ \citep{masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}.
Convection occurring within regions of strong gradients in column water vapour that mark the edges of the deep tropics \citep{Mapes2018, masunagaMechanismMaintenanceSharp2020} can differ substantially from convection occurring near the centre of the ITCZ.
Similarly, the zonal organisation of the ITCZ is associated with contrasting vertical motion profiles \citep{TODOpoujolBonyInRevision}. 
% TODO: replace citation key; preprint must be available at submission
These differences may even reverse the sign of the net energy export by the circulation, potentially explaining regional differences in energy export efficiency identified in reanalysis data \citep{backGeographicVariabilityExport2006}.
Recent work on the doldrums further illustrates the uncertainty surrounding the vertical motion field in the ITCZ.
These mesoscale regions of weak surface winds have traditionally been explained as a consequence of the rising air in the ITCZ.
And, although they lie in this region of mean ascent, they appear to occur during periods of local subsidence \citep{windmillerCalmVariableInner2024}.

Mesoscale structures also influence the thermodynamic properties of the atmosphere. Convective aggregation affects entrainment and detrainment \citep{beckerEstimatingBulkEntrainment2018}, thereby altering the vertical distribution of humidity and air temperature \citep[e.g.,][]{singhInfluenceEntrainmentThermal2013}.
Changes in aggregation are further linked to systematic changes in clouds and radiation: more aggregated convection is typically associated with fewer high clouds and a drier troposphere, resulting in increased radiative cooling \citep[e.g.,][]{Tobin2012, bonyObservedModulationTropical2020}.
In the tropical Atlantic, radiation is influenced by aerosol particles transported within the Saharan Air Layer, whose dust and humidity modify radiative heating rates and may affect cloud formation \citep{gutlebenImpactsWaterVapor2019}.

The PERCUSION campaign was designed to quantify the effects of mesoscale structures and to improve the understanding of the processes that drive them. Tropical convection is known to be organised by large-scale disturbances such as equatorial waves \citep[e.g.,][]{n.takayabuLargeScaleCloudDisturbances1994} and the Madden--Julian Oscillation \citep{maddenDetection4050Day1971, maddenDescriptionGlobalScaleCirculation1972}.
In addition to these large-scale drivers, processes acting on smaller spatial scales may contribute to the organisation of convection.
In idealised simulations with homogeneous initial and boundary conditions, convection that initially develops at random locations can spontaneously aggregate \citep{nakajimaNumericalExperimentsConcerning1988, Bretherton2005}. Depending on the model configuration and the stage of aggregation, a variety of feedbacks have been proposed to explain this behaviour, including convection-humidity feedbacks mediated by detrainment, cloud-radiative feedbacks, and radiatively driven circulations that transport humidity up-gradient \citep[reviewed by][]{Wing2017}.
On yet smaller spatial scales, clouds can trigger the formation of new convection through cold pools, linking successive generations of clouds and organising convection along gust fronts, notably in squall lines \citep{Rotunno1988, Weisman2004}, as well as along the edges of convectively active regions \citep{Nolan2016, windmillerConvectionEdge2019}.
Other processes have been argued to drive enhanced convection at the edges of the deep tropics \citep{masunagaRadiativeInvigorationTropical2018, masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}.
Assessing the relative importance of these mechanisms in the real atmosphere remains an open challenge \citep{mullerSpontaneousAggregationConvective2022}.

To this end, PERCUSION collected data from the interior and margins of the Atlantic ITCZ, as well as from the surrounding regions of subsidence, first near Sal in the Republic of Cabo Verde in the eastern basin, and later east of Barbados in the western basin. Rather than targeting individual cloud systems, the flights were designed to sample across the ITCZ around locations defined by EarthCARE overpasses (Sect.~\ref{sec:campaign_design}).

A particular focus of PERCUSION was the measurement of quantities central to the tropical energy budget that are rarely accessible observationally. These include profiles of vertical motion derived from dropsonde circles \citep{bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019, gloecknerBEACHBarbadosEastern2026}, which constrain the overturning circulation of the tropical atmosphere, and direct observational estimates of convective entrainment, one of the most important yet poorly observed processes shaping convection and its environment \citep{sherwoodSpreadModelClimate2014}. PERCUSION also collected detailed observations of tropical ice clouds, including their microphysical properties and integrated quantities such as ice water path, to quantify their radiative feedback, which remains a major uncertainty in climate projections \citep{sherwoodAssessmentEarthClimate2020}.
Many of these measurements rely on retrieval techniques and analysis methods that were further developed during and after the campaign, and the resulting datasets are being distributed using new approaches designed to facilitate community access and cross-platform analysis.

The remainder of the paper describes the measurement strategy (Section~\ref{sec:campaign_design}) and the instrumentation and data distribution (Section~\ref{sec:data}), followed by the implementation of the strategy during the campaign (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). Section~\ref{sec:overview_plots} shows how the observations sample the ITCZ and presents selected case studies, before concluding in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

During ORCESTRA, PERCUSION followed a largely generic flight strategy to sample the Atlantic ITCZ. 
By this we mean that flight planning followed a predefined set of rules that were applied consistently throughout the campaign. 
The generic flight strategy is described in this section, while adjustments required by airspace restrictions, weather conditions and other constraints are discussed in Sect.~\ref{sec:implementation}.
The central idea was to sample the structure of the ITCZ in an ITCZ-based reference frame, i.e. to systematically sample the properties at the edges and in the centre of the ITCZ rather than, for example, targeting the locations of deepest convection. Following \cite{Mapes2018}, the ITCZ was primarily identified through its high column water vapour (CWV) content, with its edges defined either by the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour or by the strongest CWV gradient. In cases where the CWV field was fragmented, or where separate regions of high CWV occurred north of the ITCZ, surface wind direction was additionally considered following \cite{windmillerInnerLifeAtlantic2024}.
%The location and width of the Atlantic ITCZ can change by several degrees within the course of days \cite{Chen1982, Frank1983}. 

A summary of rules used for flight planning, together with an example flight, is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:generic_flight_east}. 
In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item flights were scheduled on days when flight operations could be conducted in a region and manner that would allow for an EarthCARE overpass,
\item flights took place during daytime, because several of the passive remote sensing instruments require sunlight; nighttime overpasses of EarthCARE were therefore not targeted,
\item HALO took the most direct route to the EarthCARE ground track after takeoff and remained on it, often for the majority of the flight, always for at least twenty minutes before and after the satellite underpass,
\item when the dominant CWV gradient was oriented north--south: dropsonde circles with a radius of about 130\,\unit{km} were placed at the southern and northern edges and in the centre of the ITCZ,
\item when the dominant CWV gradient was oriented east--west (occasionally in the western basin): dropsonde circles of the same size were arranged along this gradient,
\item the order in which the circles were flown was not fixed but depended on the timing of the EarthCARE underpass and the length of the flight,
\item whenever possible, HALO operated at the highest feasible altitude, ideally above the tropical high-cloud layer,
\item coordinated observations with other platforms were conducted whenever possible, including overpasses of and circles around RV METEOR and the SAFIRE ATR-42, the latter with a smaller radius (about 70\,\unit{km}) to optimise the comparison between the circle-averaged quantities and the ATR in situ measurements along its roughly 200\,\unit{km} long legs, as well as overpasses of the Cape Verde Atmospheric Observatory (CVAO) and the Barbados Cloud Observatory (BCO).
\end{itemize}

In these cases, the dropsonde circles were aligned east-west to sample along the strongest column water vapour gradients but without crossing the ITCZ.

The rationale behind the different elements of the flight strategy was as follows. Flying along the EarthCARE ground track served several purposes.
First, it contributed to the calibration and validation of EarthCARE observations and facilitated the extrapolation of our results to other regions and periods.
Second, HALO often flew along the track in both directions, although this became harder in the western basin. Together with the near-instantaneous cross-section from EarthCARE, this yielded up to three cross-sections of the ITCZ. Only at the underpass did HALO and EarthCARE sample the same location at the same time; elsewhere along the track, the cross-sections were taken up to several hours apart.
This configuration provides a means of assessing how strongly the mesoscale structure evolves on timescales of a few hours.
Third, flying directly to the EarthCARE ground track after takeoff, rather than taking the shortest route to the ITCZ edge, ensured that HALO also sampled the full transition from the subsiding region to the ITCZ centre.

The placement of dropsonde circles at the ITCZ edges, or along the dominant CWV gradient, was motivated by the desire to connect the observations as directly as possible to previous studies. In particular, the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour has been identified as a transition between the deep tropics and the surrounding subsiding regions, while edge intensification refers to precipitation maxima occurring preferentially near this boundary. Vertical velocity measurements in these regions therefore enable a direct comparison with the mechanisms discussed by \cite{Mapes2018}, \cite{masunagaMechanismMaintenanceSharp2020}, and \cite{masunagaEdgeIntensificationEastern2023}.

More generally, defining the ITCZ in terms of CWV provides a natural connection to idealised studies of convective self-aggregation \citep[e.g.,][]{craigCoarseningModelSelforganization2013, Wing2017a}, in which the moist margins mark the regions where up-gradient moisture transport by a shallow circulation \citep{Muller2012}, if present, is expected to be most efficient. It also allows a direct comparison with \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018}, who reconstructed the overturning circulation of the tropical atmosphere in moisture space from five years of ground-based measurements at the BCO and reanalysis data, exploiting the fact that the BCO samples different parts of moisture space over the course of the year.

\subsection{Instrumentation}
\label{sec:instrumentation}

HALO operated in an updated version of the “flying cloud observatory” configuration described by \cite{stevensHighAltitudeLongRangeAircraft2019}. This configuration combines active and passive remote-sensing instruments with in situ measurements from dropsondes, providing a comprehensive view of the atmospheric state, including its thermodynamic and dynamical structure as well as the microphysical properties of aerosol particles, clouds, and precipitation. The complete configuration used during PERCUSION is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:instrumentation}.
% TODO: add HALO endurance sentence
A key component of the remote-sensing suite is the HALO Microwave Package (HAMP), which combines a suite of passive microwave radiometers with a cloud radar \citep{mechHAMPMicrowavePackage2014, ewaldCalibration35GHz2019}. The passive radiometers consist of three downward-looking modules with 26 frequencies in different bands between 22.24 and 183.31\,\unit{GHz} $\pm$ 12.5\,\unit{GHz} that sample water vapour and oxygen absorption bands, as well as window channels. These measurements provide information on the different thermodynamic phases of water and their vertically integrated amounts, such as column water vapour, liquid water path and ice water path. The nadir-pointing microwave cloud and precipitation radar operates at 35.5\,\unit{GHz} (Ka band) and is suitable for penetrating columns with heavy precipitation. It measures radar reflectivity, reflectivity-weighted Doppler velocity, and the linear-depolarisation ratio to determine target shape.

The lidar system WALES (WAter vapour Lidar Experiment in Space) was developed as an airborne demonstrator for a mission proposal within the European Space Agency's (ESA) Earth Explorer programme. WALES operates at four wavelengths near 935\,\unit{nm} to deliver profiles of the water vapour mixing ratio below the aircraft at a vertical resolution of [X]\,\unit{m} and a horizontal resolution of [Y]\,\unit{km}, using the DIfferential Absorption Lidar (DIAL) technique \citep{wirthAirborneMultiwavelengthWater2009}. Additionally, it has backscatter lidar channels at 532\,\unit{nm} and 1064\,\unit{nm}; at 532\,\unit{nm}, aerosol depolarisation and extinction can be retrieved using the High Spectral Resolution Lidar (HSRL) method. At 1064\,\unit{nm}, only attenuated backscatter measurements are possible. % TODO: WALES resolutions

Radiance and irradiance measurements are obtained using the Spectral Modular Airborne Radiation Measurement System (SMART), a suite of passive spectrometers measuring spectral irradiances and radiances within the visible and near-infrared wavelength ranges (0.3--2.2\,\unit{\mu m}) \citep{wendischAirborneSpectralAlbedometer2001, wendischACRIDICONCHUVACampaign2016}. The irradiance measurements allow the determination of the spectral cloud top albedo, while the spectral radiance observations are used for cloud flagging and to derive microphysical properties, including cloud optical depth and effective droplet radius.
The spectrometer of the Munich Aerosol Cloud Scanner (specMACS) complements these measurements by providing hyperspectral imaging in a wavelength range similar to that of SMART (0.4--2.5\,\unit{\mu m}; \citealp{ewaldDesignCharacterizationSpecMACS2016}), combined with measurements of four two-dimensional polarisation-resolving RGB cameras \citep{weberPolarizationUpgradeSpecMACS2024}, two downward-looking and two newly installed sideward-facing, such that an overall across-track field of view of 220$^{\circ}$ is covered.
The specMACS measurements allow the characterisation of clouds with respect to their macroscopic geometry and their microphysical structure in terms of the cloud droplet size distribution, cloud optical depth and thermodynamic phase.

This imaging capability is extended into the thermal infrared by the Video airbornE Longwave Observations within siX channels (VELOX) instrument, which captures high-resolution two-dimensional fields of upward terrestrial radiance across six spectral bands between 7.7 and 12.0\,\unit{\mu m}, providing information on cloud top brightness temperature, cloud mask/fraction, and cloud top altitude \citep{schaferVELOXNewThermal2022}.
To quantify the cloud radiative effect and the energy budget, HALO utilises the Broadband AirCrAft RaDiometer Instrumentation (BACARDI), which consists of two sets of pyranometers and pyrgeometers mounted on the upper and lower fuselage to measure upward and downward solar (0.3--3\,\unit{\mu m}) and thermal-infrared (3--100\,\unit{\mu m}) irradiances, respectively \citep{ehrlichNewAirborneBroadband2023}.

In situ observations are primarily obtained using dropsondes deployed with the Airborne Vertical Atmospheric Profiling System (AVAPS). Vaisala RD-41 dropsondes provide measurements of temperature, humidity, pressure, and GPS-derived horizontal winds throughout the atmospheric column. Additionally, for each circle, area-averaged estimates of large-scale divergence, vorticity and vertical velocity within the sampled air masses are calculated from horizontal winds \citep{bonyMeasuringAreaAveragedVertical2019}.
A detailed description of the PERCUSION dropsonde dataset, including data processing, quality control, and the derivation of circle-averaged quantities, is provided by \cite{gloecknerBEACHBarbadosEastern2026}.
Additional in situ measurements of temperature, humidity, and pressure at flight level are provided by HALO's core instrument: BAHAMAS (BAsic HAlo Measurement And Sensor system; \citealp{krautstrunkTransitionFALCONHALO2012}).

\subsection{Data distribution}
\label{sec:data_distribution}

As part of PERCUSION, a decentralised data distribution strategy was introduced for HALO to ensure that scientific datasets are openly accessible, verifiable and resilient. Moving away from traditional central servers, this approach uses the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS), which operates on two core principles:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Content addressing: datasets are identified and referenced by a unique, cryptographically secure content identifier (CID) derived from the content itself, which acts as an immutable fingerprint. Any change to the data therefore results in a new CID, ensuring that any analysis can be traced back to exactly the data it used.
    \item Data lookup by CID in a peer-to-peer network: IPFS provides protocols to find and exchange data in a network. As the CID is sufficient to identify the data and verify their integrity, any computer can serve the data.
\end{itemize}

We first used the peer-to-peer capability of IPFS for on-site data sharing during the field campaign.
To increase retrievability and ensure long-term availability, we continue to use IPFS with multiple independent, well-connected server locations (currently the German Climate Computing Center (DKRZ), the Gesellschaft für wissenschaftliche Datenverarbeitung mbH Göttingen (GWDG) and other partner institutions), so that there is no single point of failure.
Furthermore, users can automatically cache and share retrieved datasets, providing fast access for repeated use.

To make the data findable, we built the ORCESTRA Data Browser (\url{https://browser.orcestra-campaign.org/}), which provides search and preview functionality for the datasets. The browser extracts the metadata on demand directly from the original datasets on IPFS, so that metadata do not have to be duplicated or maintained separately.
To further enhance scholarly traceability, DOIs with embedded CIDs can be provided for each dataset.

To enable an easy and reproducible analysis of the key elements of the flights, a flight segmentation system was developed. Based on that system, the individual flights were categorised manually. Both segments and events are categorised according to their 'kind'. They are organised hierarchically and are not mutually exclusive. The results of the flight segmentation are provided in YAML format, which is both human- and machine-readable, can be read with standard tools in most programming languages, and can be easily accessed in Python via the \textit{pyorcestra} package.

Throughout the campaign, systematic adaptations to the generic flight strategy were necessary. These included adjustments to enable coordinated measurements with other platforms, responses to weather conditions (e.g.~avoiding severe thunderstorms), airspace restrictions, technical issues, on-board instrument calibration and validation, and targeted manoeuvres to test new retrieval techniques. While these factors led primarily to minor adjustments in the eastern basin, more substantial modifications were required in the western Atlantic. In particular, the combination of stricter airspace restrictions and a more variable CWV field necessitated changes to the sampling strategy. In this region, the strongest CWV gradients were frequently oriented in an east--west direction. In some cases, these gradients were either not clearly identifiable or located too far east to be reachable within the available flight time. Consequently, dropsonde circles in the western Atlantic were often aligned either east--west or in mixed east--west and north--south configurations. Nevertheless, even in cases with predominantly east--west sampling, parts of the flight track remained aligned in the north--south direction in order to maintain sufficient overlap with the EarthCARE ground track for calibration and validation purposes.

\section{Overview plots}
\label{sec:overview_plots}


In the following, we present selected measurements collected during the 23 PERCUSION research flights in the tropical Atlantic. We first demonstrate that the observations sampled the Atlantic ITCZ, by examining the measurements in moisture space. We then illustrate the day-to-day variability of the ITCZ through three case studies: one at the edge of the ITCZ, and two near its centre, one characterised by deep convection and one with little deep convection and weak surface winds.

\subsection{Sampling the ITCZ} 

We start by showing that the research flights sampled the Atlantic ITCZ, including its centre, edges and adjacent subsiding regions. To this end, we examine the sampling relative to the moist margin, defined by the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour, and the interior of the ITCZ, defined by CWV values exceeding 48\,\unit{mm}. We then show that the observations capture the transition from subsiding to ascending conditions and from infrequent to frequent deep convection, corresponding to the transition from outside to inside the ITCZ.

\subsubsection{Sampling in moisture space}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/transition_circles.pdf}
\caption{Sampling of the ITCZ in moisture space. The dotted horizontal line marks CWV = 48\,\unit{mm}. (a) Mean CWV of each dropsonde circle, calculated from all its dropsondes (dots), and the range of CWV across the circle (vertical lines). Dark (light) blue indicates whether or not the circle crosses the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour. The vertical line marks the transfer flight from Sal to Barbados on 6 September 2024. (b) Probability density functions of CWV from (blue) the dropsondes and (grey) HAMP. The mean is shown in black, individual flights in blue to green colours.}

As described in Sect.~\ref{sec:campaign_design}, the 48\,\unit{mm} CWV contour was used to guide the placement of dropsonde circles both at the edges of the ITCZ and in its centre.
Figure~\ref{fig:transition}a shows CWV calculated from the dropsondes released in these circles.
Of the 87 dropsonde circles with sufficient data to calculate circle mean values, 51 contain dropsondes with CWV both below and above 48\,\unit{mm} and thus straddle the contour (edge circles), 35 contain only dropsondes with CWV above 48\,\unit{mm} (centre circles), and only one contains only dropsondes with CWV below 48\,\unit{mm}.
Flights from Sal (all flights before 6 September 2024) typically included two circles at the ITCZ edges, one in the ITCZ centre, and one coordinated with the ATR. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}a, eight of the eleven flights in the eastern tropical Atlantic sampled both ITCZ edges and the ITCZ centre.
This pattern changed in the western basin (from 7 September 2024 onwards), where the flights differed substantially (see Sect.~\ref{sec:implementation}). None of the flights sampled both ITCZ edges and the centre. Nevertheless, seven of the eleven flights included at least one circle at an ITCZ edge and one in the centre.

Our sampling differed between the eastern and the western Atlantic largely because the CWV field itself differed between the two basins.
These differences are evident in the mean and per-flight meridional cross-sections of CWV from HAMP, computed in 0.1\textdegree{} latitude bins (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}c, d).
In the east (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}c), each flight shows a CWV plateau, i.e. a latitude band of roughly constant, high CWV, whose extent varies from flight to flight but lies within about 4 to 12\,\unit{^{\circ}N}.
At the southern edge of the plateau, CWV drops sharply on all but one flight.
At the northern edge, it does so on only about half of the flights.
On some of the days with a sharp northern drop, a second CWV maximum appears north of about 12\,\unit{^{\circ}N}.
Accordingly, the mean cross-section for the eastern domain shows a CWV plateau between about 7 and 10\,\unit{^{\circ}N}, with CWV decreasing more gradually towards the north than towards the south.

In the west (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}d), most flights also show a CWV plateau, but its value varies between flights, from values similar to those in the east down to about 48\,\unit{mm}.
As a result, the mean CWV peaks at only about 55\,\unit{mm}, compared with about 60\,\unit{mm} in the east.
The plateau edges are also less clear as only one flight shows a sharp drop at the southern edge and only one other at the northern edge.
There are two reasons for this.
First, airspace restrictions often confined the western flights to a narrower latitude band, so that the edges may lie outside the sampled range.
Second, when the plateau value was close to the 48\,\unit{mm} threshold used to define the ITCZ edges, the edges were difficult to identify and therefore to sample.

Finally, Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}b shows the full distribution of CWV values measured by the dropsondes and by HAMP. It highlights that the observations were concentrated near the ITCZ edge and within the moist ITCZ. In particular, the distribution exhibits a pronounced peak near 57\,\unit{mm}, close to the peak at 55\,\unit{mm} reported by \cite{Mapes2018}. The dropsonde-derived CWV also shows a weaker secondary peak near 50\,\unit{mm}. This differs from \cite{Mapes2018}, who found a local minimum around 48\,\unit{mm}. This likely reflects our targeted sampling of the 48\,\unit{mm} contour. Consistent with our objectives, the majority of our observations were collected in the region of high CWV but also allow for analysis of the adjacent drier subtropical regions as 24$\%$ of both the dropsonde and HAMP measurements fall below 48\,\unit{mm} and our observations include CWV values as low as 30\,\unit{mm}.  
Finally, the close agreement between the CWV distributions derived from dropsondes and measured by HAMP indicates that the dropsonde circles adequately capture the variability observed throughout the flights.


\subsubsection{ITCZ in moisture space}

    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/itcz_cwv_space.pdf}

(a) Cloud occurrence frequency from the HAMP radar (yellow to brown), relative humidity from WALES (blue) and frequency of stereoscopically derived cloud-top heights from specMACS (black contours: dotted 0.01\%, dashed 0.02\%, solid 0.04\%). The previous section showed that our measurements span a broad range of CWV, from the dry regions adjacent to the ITCZ, across its moist margins, into its moist interior. We now ask whether this sampling also captures the transition from the dry, subsiding environment to the moist, ascending ITCZ. To this end, we examine the measurements in moisture space rather than averaging them by latitude: because CWV provides a dynamical definition of the moist tropics, deep convection is expected to be confined to high CWV even when the ITCZ widens, shifts meridionally or breaks up into more complex structures.

Figure~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv} shows how cloudiness, humidity, radiation, and the large-scale circulation change across this transition. Cloud occurrence frequency (Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}a) is estimated as the fraction of HAMP radar observations with an equivalent radar reflectivity factor exceeding $10^{-5}$\,\unit{mm^{6}\,m^{-3}}. Relative humidity outside clouds is calculated from the water vapour number density measured by WALES, the temperature from the meteorological analysis included in the WALES product, and the Bolton formulation of the saturation vapour pressure over liquid water. Figure~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}b shows upwelling longwave radiation flux (ULRF) from BACARDI and ice water path (IWP) from the HAMP passive radiometers. 
% Add details for specMACS
All quantities in Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}a and b are averaged in 1\,\unit{mm} CWV bins, using the CWV measured by HAMP, between 44 and 62\,\unit{mm}, the 10th and 90th percentiles of the observed CWV distribution.


As expected, clouds become deeper and more frequent with increasing CWV. Shallow trade-wind clouds first become detectable at around 50\,\unit{mm}, although their low occurrence frequency at lower CWV likely reflects the limited sensitivity of the radar to non-precipitating low clouds far from the radar (about $-30$\,\unit{dBZ} at a range of 13\,\unit{km}; \citealp{mechHAMPMicrowavePackage2014a}).
% Add specMACS
Above a CWV of about 55\,\unit{mm}, there is a rapid increase in deep convection. This is apparent in a decrease in the upwelling longwave radiation flux and increases in ice water path and cloud occurrence frequency.
Although cloud occurrence increases throughout the column, a local maximum persists near 5.5\,\unit{km}. Together with the maxima associated with trade-wind clouds and deep convection, it is reminiscent of the trimodal structure of tropical convection \citep{johnsonTrimodalCharacteristicsTropical1999}.
The observed sharp increase of deep convection with CWV is consistent with the well-documented sharp increase of precipitation with CWV \citep[e.g.,][]{Bretherton2004, Peters2006}.

Figure~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}c shows the overturning circulation in moisture space together with the moist static energy profiles.
The moisture-space streamfunction $\Psi$ was introduced by \citet{Bretherton2005} for a closed domain and first applied to observations by \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018}, who combined observed CWV with vertical motion from reanalyses.
Here, $\Psi$ is calculated from observed CWV and observed vertical motion, to our knowledge for the first time.
This is the first time that $\Psi$ is calculated using observations of vertical motion (rather than reanalysis data).
To this end, all circles with vertical velocity profiles ($N = 87$) are sorted from highest to lowest CWV, and $\Psi$ is calculated as $\Psi_i(z) = \Psi_{i-1}(z) + \overline{\rho}(z)\,w_i(z)/N$, with $\Psi_0 = 0$, where $w_i$ is the circle-mean vertical velocity of the $i$th circle and $\overline{\rho}$ is the mean density profile of all circles.
Unlike \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018}, who start the integration at the dry end, we start at the moist end because the circles mainly sample the moistest rather than the driest region of the overturning circulation. The value of $\Psi$ at the dry end then corresponds to the mass flux that descends in the unsampled dry regions.
Each circle receives equal weight, which assumes that the circles approximately sample the CWV distribution of the region, although the ITCZ edges are somewhat over-represented (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}b).
The streamfunction (Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}c) reaches a maximum of about 6\,\unit{g\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}} at around 8\,\unit{km} and 56\,\unit{mm}. Net ascent in the upper troposphere is thus confined to circles with CWV above about 56\,\unit{mm}, which roughly coincides with the transition to frequent deep convection in Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}a and b. 
The overturning circulation is broadly consistent with those that \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018} reconstructed from several reanalyses, except that the inflow in our observations is more confined to the boundary layer.

The shading in Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}c shows the moist static energy $h$ from the dropsondes, averaged in moisture space in the same way as the variables in Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}a and b. It is calculated as $h = c_\mathrm{pd} T + \ell_\mathrm{v} q + g z$ \citep{mayMetPyPythonPackage2016a}, where $c_\mathrm{pd}$ is the specific heat capacity of dry air at constant pressure, $T$ is temperature, the specific enthalpy of vaporization $\ell_\mathrm{v}$ (often called the latent heat of vaporization), $q$ is specific humidity, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $z$ is height.
As in \citet{Bretherton2005} and \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018}, Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}c visualizes the energy transport associated with the overturning circulation.
Compared to \citet{schulzObservingTropicalAtmosphere2018}, who sampled the seasonal rather than spatial variation, with regions of deep convection more similar to what we sample in the west, the stronger confinement of the inflow to the boundary layer, where $h$ is high, makes the circulation less efficient at exporting energy. 
When the analysis is done separately for the eastern and western domain (not shown), the inflow layer appears deeper and the circulation more efficient in the west, whereas an additional shallow circulation becomes apparent in the east.
These results need additional analysis, especially given the small number of circles and the limited sampling of the dry end of the circulation. However, they illustrate how the data collected during PERCUSION might be used to assess observationally how efficiently the ITCZ exports energy.

Finally, we note two limitations of the moisture-space perspective.
First, it hides how sharp the ITCZ edges can be in physical space. We define the ITCZ as the region with CWV above 48\,\unit{mm}, the moist margin of \citet{Mapes2018}, yet deep convection becomes frequent only near 60\,\unit{mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}), consistent with the precipitation pick-up found by \citet{Bretherton2004} and \citet{Peters2006}. In physical space, however, the two thresholds lie close together. Where CWV crosses 48\,\unit{mm}, it increases by a median of about 6\,\unit{mm} per degree latitude (not shown) and thus reaches 60\,\unit{mm} within about 2\textdegree{}, or roughly 200\,\unit{km}. This matches the distance between the 48\,\unit{mm} contour and the precipitation peak reported by \citet{Mapes2018}. Because the gradient is similar for thresholds between about 45 and 55\,\unit{mm} (not shown), a range of CWV values might have been suitable to identify the edges of the ITCZ during the campaign.

Second, CWV alone does not determine whether deep convection occurs.
A better predictor may be the lower-tropospheric buoyancy $B_L$ of an entraining plume \citep{ahmedReverseEngineeringTropical2018, ahmedDeepConvectiveAdjustment2020}, which accounts for the vertical distribution of water vapour and temperature.
Figure~\ref{fig:beyond_moisture_space} shows $B_L$ calculated from the dropsondes following \citet{ahmedDeepConvectiveAdjustment2020}.
In the west, $B_L$ is strongly correlated with CWV.
In the east, $B_L$ at a given CWV decreases northward, suggesting conditions that are less favourable for convection.
This is consistent with the ice water path increasing and the upwelling longwave radiation decreasing markedly more slowly with CWV in measurements from the eastern flights north of 12\unit{{\degree}}\,N than in the full dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}b).

\subsection{Day-to-day variability of the ITCZ}

Aggregating measurements in moisture space, as in the previous section, discards temporal and spatial information. A guiding idea of PERCUSION, however, was to collect ``snapshots'' of the ITCZ in physical space that capture the day-to-day variability of its mesoscale structure. Here we showcase our observations in three such snapshots: one circle at the edge of the high-CWV region and two in its center that have similar CWV but contrasting convection. 
Each snapshot is accompanied by output from 1.25\,km ICON limited-area simulations run alongside the campaign \citep{fievetTropicalConvectionStormresolving2026}.

It consisted of a mostly straight connection (straight\_leg) between Sal and the EarthCARE ground track (ec\_track), three circles (circles) placed at the margins and centre of the ITCZ, and a coordination circle with the ATR (atr\_coordination).

Flight \textit{HALO-20240903a} is the same flight shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:generic_flight_east}, whose satellite image already shows signs of high convective activity, especially near the centre of the ITCZ. This is well captured by the LAM simulation, which shows a region of high outgoing shortwave radiation at the centre of the ITCZ, indicating the presence of convective clouds (Fig.~\ref{fig:deep_conv_itcz}a). The high convective activity is also apparent in the radar measurements, which show deep convective activity throughout the central ITCZ circle (Fig.~\ref{fig:deep_conv_itcz}c). While the frequent presence of deep convection significantly limited how much of the vertical structure could be observed by WALES, the measurements that were possible above cloud top show what appears to be an imprint of the convective activity on the humidity field (see, for example, the moist layer and its relation to the underlying cloud at around 15:25 UTC in Fig.~\ref{fig:deep_conv_itcz}c).

As might be expected, the vertical velocity profile derived from the dropsondes shows that air within this circle is, on average, ascending (Fig.~\ref{fig:deep_conv_itcz}b). The strongest ascent is observed at around 10\,\unit{km} altitude, with a maximum exceeding 5\,\unit{cm\,s^{-1}} -- significantly higher than the maximum ascent rate observed at the ITCZ edge in Fig.~\ref{fig:at_edge_of_itcz}b. While the top-heaviness of the profile is consistent with the presence of deep convection, it also shows a layer of near-zero vertical velocity between 4 and 6\,\unit{km}, and a further layer of ascending motion reaching up to 4\,\unit{km} altitude, consistent with convection in different stages of development within the circle.
The ITCZ sampled here fits well with the conventional picture of the ITCZ (see also Fig.~\ref{fig:itcz_cwv}): high CWV, frequent deep convection in different stages of its lifecycle, and strong ascent at the centre.

\subsubsection{Wind-still ITCZ}
Figure~\ref{fig:weak_winds} shows flight \textit{HALO-20240907a} on 7 September, which was over the western Atlantic, and based out of Barbados. As in the previous case, three large dropsonde circles were placed along the EarthCARE track to sample the ITCZ's structure; however, the longer transit to the track meant that only a single north--south crossing could be flown. No coordination circle was flown with the ATR which remained in the eastern Atlantic throughout the campaign or with the RV METEOR which was not yet within reach.

Figure~\ref{fig:weak_winds} shows flight \textit{HALO-20240907a} on 7 September 2024, which took place over the western Atlantic and was based in Barbados. As in the previous case, three large dropsonde circles were placed along the EarthCARE track to sample the structure of the ITCZ; however, the longer transit to the track meant that only a single north--south crossing could be flown. No coordination circle was flown with either the ATR, which remained in the eastern Atlantic throughout the campaign, or the RV METEOR, which was not yet within reach.

As in Section~\ref{sec:cloudy_itcz}, we here focus on the dropsonde circle flown in the centre of the ITCZ. CWV in this circle is comparably high, at 60.16\,\unit{mm}, yet both the vertical velocity profile -- close to zero up to about 5\,\unit{km} -- and the radar reflectivity indicate very little convective activity: no convection extended throughout the column, and what did occur remained largely confined below about 7\,\unit{km}.

Rather than deep convection, the centre of the ITCZ on this flight was characterised by an extended region of low surface winds, consistent with doldrums conditions. The dropsonde-derived mean wind speed in the lowest 100\,\unit{m} is 2.5\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}, below the commonly used doldrums threshold of 3\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}; indeed, 9 of the 13 dropsondes released in the circle measured near-surface wind speeds below this threshold. This is also consistent with the LAM simulation, which shows extended regions of low wind speed across the ITCZ on that day. 

Low surface wind speeds were also evident in the specMACS measurements. Using a newly developed retrieval, specMACS can, under suitable conditions, estimate surface wind speed from the specular reflection of sunlight on the ocean surface: lower wind speeds produce a smoother sea surface and thus a more pronounced specular reflection. This is illustrated by the two near-cloud-free specMACS images in Fig.~\ref{fig:weak_winds}d: the image taken at 16:06:29 UTC shows comparatively weak specular reflection, corresponding to a retrieved wind speed of 3.5\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}, while the image at 16:56:29 UTC shows a far more pronounced reflection, corresponding to a retrieved wind speed below 1.0\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}.

Convective activity in the centre of the ITCZ was limited but not absent. A further specMACS RGB image, taken at 16:16:03 UTC, shows one of the convective clouds that reached up to about 7\,\unit{km} altitude; the accompanying specMACS retrievals in Fig.~\ref{fig:weak_winds}d illustrate its macro- and microphysical properties. 
The cloud top heights are derived using a stereographic approach \citep{Koelling2019,Volkmer2024a}.
Liquid cloud properties in terms of the effective droplet size and width of the cloud droplet size distribution (in particular effective radius (reff) and effective variance (veff)) are derived from polarised measurements of the cloudbow \citep{portgeHighspatialresolutionRetrievalCloud2023}.
Since the microphysics retrieval requires the observation of cloud targets under scattering angles of $135 ^{\circ}$ to $165 ^{\circ}$, the reff and veff are derived only for parts of the image. 
The large cloud-top effective radii (reff between about 15\,\unit{\mu m} and 30\,\unit{\mu m}) and broad droplet size distributions (large veff) hint at the presence of rain beneath, consistent with the radar measurements at the time the image was taken.

\conclusions
\label{sec:conclusion}

Exactly fifty years after GATE, the largest field campaign ever organised, ORCESTRA took place in the tropical Atlantic in August and September 2024. A key motivation was to revisit the impact and drivers of mesoscale structures in the deep tropics. This paper summarises the contribution of PERCUSION, the ORCESTRA sub-campaign utilising the German research aircraft HALO.

The aim of PERCUSION was to collect vertically resolved snapshots of the mesoscale structure of the Atlantic ITCZ and its adjacent subsiding regions. Rather than targeting individual cloud systems, we followed a generic flight strategy, in which the ITCZ was identified dynamically as the region of high CWV. HALO crossed the ITCZ meridionally, flying back and forth along the EarthCARE ground track. Dropsonde circles were placed at both edges and in the centre of the ITCZ. Across the 23 research flights, eleven from Sal, eleven from Barbados and one transfer flight, the median flight duration was just under nine hours and the median flight altitude above 13 km. While the eastern flights largely followed this strategy, airspace restrictions and a less clearly defined ITCZ meant that flights in the western basin were often oriented zonally rather than meridionally. Whenever feasible, measurements were coordinated with other ORCESTRA platforms, including the SAFIRE ATR-42, RV METEOR, the Cape Verde Atmospheric Observatory and the Barbados Cloud Observatory.

Configured as a flying cloud observatory, HALO combined remote-sensing measurements of clouds, precipitation, water vapour, aerosols and radiation with dropsonde profiles of the thermodynamic state and horizontal winds. Dropsondes circles additionally yield area-averaged vertical motion. Composited in moisture space, the observations capture the transition from regions largely free of deep convection to regions with frequent deep convection, at CWV values similar to those found in previous studies. Applying the same compositing to the vertical motion profiles yields a tentative estimate of the overturning circulation of the ITCZ in moisture space, based, for the first time, on observed rather than inferred vertical motion.

Beyond this mean picture, the mesoscale structure of the ITCZ varied considerably from day to day. Its edges were not always marked by a sharp CWV gradient, and steep gradients were not confined to the 48 mm contour. In the western basin, regions of elevated CWV were often fragmented rather than forming a coherent ITCZ. In the eastern basin, sharp gradients occurred most systematically at the southern edge, whereas the northern margin was frequently marked by a second region of high CWV, often without deep convection. Even in the centre of the ITCZ, regions with similar CWV ranged from weak winds and little convection to vigorous convection of different types and life stages.
One of the questions that motivated the campaign was whether the energy export by the overturning circulation depends on the mesoscale structure of the ITCZ. Combining the overturning circulation with profiles of moist static energy now allows this export to be estimated, provided the limited number of observations permits a robust estimate, and to be compared, for example, between cases with and without edge intensification or between the eastern and western basins. Relating the overturning circulation to radiation, whether measured directly or inferred from thermodynamic and cloud profiles, further provides a link to idealised studies of convective aggregation. In the low-wind case in the centre of the ITCZ, we find no evidence for the strong ascent traditionally associated with the doldrums, but a vertical motion profile more complex than simple descent. At the northern margin, where the absence of deep convection appears linked to the vertical structure of humidity and temperature, the observations also reveal an intricate vertical structure of aerosols, humidity and clouds, offering an opportunity to study their interactions. Finally, observations of tropical high clouds, including their ice water path and their spatially resolved radiative and microphysical properties, make it possible to ask whether these properties change with the degree of mesoscale aggregation.% Entrainment: circles around individual convective systems; new retrievals under development.

%Other questions emerged only from the observations themselves. What sets the sharpness of the CWV gradient at the edges of the ITCZ, and is the 48 mm contour special? Why do the northern and southern edges differ so markedly, what causes the strong CWV perturbations at the northern margin, and why are these perturbations often free of deep convection?

Like any campaign overview, this paper does not aim to answer these questions but to provide the basis for addressing them. We have described the questions that guided the data collection, the data themselves, and how to access them, use the flight segmentation, and find further information on the campaign website. Although the observations are limited to a single region and season, their close links to EarthCARE, the limited-area simulations and global storm-resolving models should allow the results to be extrapolated to other regions and periods. We hope these data to be useful for a broad range of questions, not only for evaluating models and satellite retrievals, but above all for testing hypotheses about the impact and drivers of mesoscale structures in the deep tropics. Together with the GATE observations collected fifty years earlier, they also provide a rare opportunity to test hypotheses about how the tropical atmosphere responds to a changing climate.
